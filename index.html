Step 6 · Wiring · 60 min

Connect the LC29H GNSS receiver step by step

This is the most important practical setup step. Work slowly and do not connect or move jumper wires while boards are powered.

Safety before wiring

  • Disconnect USB power from both boards before changing wires.
  • Work on a non-conductive surface.
  • Keep liquids and metal objects away.
  • Touch a grounded metal object or anti-static mat before handling boards.
  • Handle boards by the edges and do not force connectors.

Step A — Set the LC29H jumpers

The yellow jumpers on the Waveshare LC29H must be set to Row B / PI-LC29H position. This routes the LC29H UART through the Raspberry Pi style header so the Tenstar can read it.

If the jumpers are in Row A / USB-LC29H mode, the Tenstar will not receive NMEA data.

Step B — Connect the external antenna

  1. Find the tiny gold IPX/U.FL connector on the LC29H board.
  2. Align the antenna plug directly above it.
  3. Press straight down gently but firmly until it clicks.
  4. Do not twist, drag sideways, or pull the cable at an angle.
  5. Place the antenna close to a window or outdoors with clear sky view.
PPS LED: the LC29H PPS LED blinks once per second when satellites are locked. It may take 1–2 minutes after power-on.

Step C — Connect the three UART wires

  • Black wire: GND to GND
  • Yellow wire: LC29H TX to Tenstar GPIO17 RX
  • Blue wire: Tenstar GPIO18 TX to LC29H RX

  • Antenna connected straight down and placed near window
  • No wires touching adjacent pins
  • Both USB cables are data-capable

  1. Power the LC29H with its own USB cable first.
  2. Wait a few seconds for the receiver to initialise.
  3. Power the Tenstar ESP32-S3 with USB-C.
  4. Select the Tenstar COM port in Arduino IDE.
  5. Upload the relevant test sketch.

Powering both at the same time is normally safe, but powering the receiver first makes troubleshooting easier.

Step 7 · Serial data · 30 min

Read raw NMEA sentences

Raw NMEA is the first real GNSS data you should see. It proves that the LC29H is powered, the UART wiring is correct, and the Tenstar can read the receiver.

$GNGGA,123519,5320.1234,N,00615.5678,W,1,08,0.9,85.4,M,48.6,M,,*47

What NMEA sentences look like

  • They begin with $.
  • The next two letters are the talker ID: GP GPS, GA Galileo, GL GLONASS, GB BeiDou, GN combined.
  • The next three letters are the sentence type: GGA, RMC, GSV, GSA, GLL, VTG.
  • Fields are separated by commas.
  • The sentence ends with *XX, a hexadecimal checksum.

Run the raw NMEA test

  1. Open NMEATestBasicLongComments.ino first. It explains the program line by line.
  2. Upload it to the Tenstar.
  3. Open Serial Monitor at 115200 baud.
  4. Look for NMEA sentences scrolling once per second.
Expected output: GGA, RMC, GSV, GSA, GLL and VTG sentences. If you see random characters, check baud rate. If you see no text, check power, jumpers, common ground and TX/RX wiring.
Step 8 · Parsing · 40 min

Parse NMEA with TinyGPSPlus

TinyGPSPlus converts NMEA text into useful variables. Instead of manually splitting comma-separated fields, you feed every incoming character into gps.encode().

while (Serial1.available()) {
  gps.encode(Serial1.read());
}

if (gps.location.isValid()) {
  double lat = gps.location.lat();
  double lon = gps.location.lng();
}

Useful functions

  • gps.location.lat() and gps.location.lng()
  • gps.altitude.meters()
  • gps.speed.kmph(), gps.speed.knots()
  • gps.course.deg()
  • gps.satellites.value()
  • gps.hdop.hdop()
  • gps.charsProcessed(), gps.failedChecksum()

Debug logic

  • charsProcessed() == 0: no GNSS characters are reaching the ESP32.
  • location.isValid() == false: receiver is sending data but has no position fix yet.
  • failedChecksum() rising: likely wrong baud rate, noise or loose wiring.
  • location.age() large: you are not calling gps.encode() frequently enough.

Coordinate format warning

NMEA latitude/longitude uses degrees and minutes, such as 5320.1234,N, meaning 53 degrees and 20.1234 minutes North. TinyGPSPlus converts this to decimal degrees automatically.

Step 11 · Theory · 50 min

Understand Galileo and OSNMA

Galileo is Europe’s civilian-controlled Global Navigation Satellite System. It provides positioning, navigation and timing services and is designed to work alongside GPS, GLONASS, BeiDou and regional systems.

Galileo facts

  • Medium Earth Orbit at approximately 23,222 km.
  • Three orbital planes.
  • Approximately 28–30 operational satellites in the full constellation.
  • Orbital period about 14 hours 4 minutes.
  • Precise atomic clocks: Passive Hydrogen Maser and Rubidium standards.

Services

  • Open Service: free positioning for everyone.
  • High Accuracy Service: free correction data for sub-metre / sub-20 cm compatible use.
  • Public Regulated Service: encrypted government use.
  • Search and Rescue: distress beacon support.
  • OSNMA: navigation message authentication.

OSNMA in plain English

OSNMA stands for Open Service Navigation Message Authentication. It helps the receiver verify that navigation messages really came from Galileo and were not spoofed. It uses the TESLA protocol and improves trustworthiness, not raw positioning accuracy.

The firmware checker sketch belongs in this section because it queries the LC29H firmware version and helps confirm whether the receiver is suitable for OSNMA-related work.

6. Anti-Static Precautions
The Tenstar board and in particular the TFT display are sensitive to electrostatic discharge (ESD). ESD
damage is permanent — always observe the following precautions.

•

Always work on the anti-static mat — place the mat on the bench before handling any board.

•

Touch the anti-static mat before picking up a board to discharge static from your body.

•

Hold boards by the edges — do not touch the TFT display surface or component pins.

•

Do not slide boards across ordinary desk surfaces or clothing.

•

Store boards in anti-static bags when not in use.

⚠ ESD Damage is Permanent

14. FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS
Q1: Do I need an external antenna?
A: No. The Waveshare LC29H has an integrated patch antenna (the gray ceramic square on the board).
This is sufficient for most applications.

Q2: How long does it take to get a GPS fix?
A:
•

Cold start (first power-on or after long time off): 30-60 seconds

•

Warm start (recent fix, ephemeris data valid): 10-30 seconds

•

Hot start (just powered back on): 1-5 seconds

The board needs to download navigation data from satellites, which takes time.

Q3: Why does my GPS show a position far from my actual location?
A: This usually happens when:
•

GPS doesn't have enough satellites (needs 4+ for 3D fix)

•

GPS is using old/invalid ephemeris data

•

Poor satellite geometry (all satellites in one direction)

Solution: Wait for more satellites to be acquired, move to better location.

Q4: Can I use this indoors?
A: GPS signals are very weak and don't penetrate buildings well. You may get:
•

No fix at all

•

Very slow fix acquisition

Q5: What is HDOP and why does it matter?
A: HDOP (Horizontal Dilution of Precision) indicates accuracy:
•

HDOP < 1.0: Excellent (rare)

•

HDOP 1.0-2.0: Good

•

HDOP 2.0-5.0: Moderate

•

HDOP > 5.0: Poor

Lower HDOP = better satellite geometry = more accurate position.

Q6: Why do I see different sentence types ($GP, $GA, $GL)?
A: The LC29H is a multi-GNSS receiver supporting:
•

$GP...: GPS (USA)

•

$GA...: Galileo (Europe)

•

$GL...: GLONASS (Russia)

•

$GB...: BeiDou (China)

•

$GN...: Combined data from multiple systems

This gives you more satellites and better accuracy.

Q7: Can I send commands to the GPS module?
A: Yes! The TX pin (blue wire, GPIO43) allows you to send configuration commands. You can:
•

Change update rate

•

Enable/disable NMEA sentence types

•

Configure OSNMA authentication

•

Set baud rate

Commands use NMEA format or UBX protocol. See Quectel LC29H documentation for details.

Q8: What is the accuracy of the LC29H?

With SBAS/DGPS: ~1 meter

•

With OSNMA (authenticated): Same accuracy but verified authentic

A: GPS has not downloaded valid almanac/ephemeris data yet. The date shown may be:
•

GPS epoch start (1980)

•

Date of last valid fix

ESP32 3.3V pin may not supply enough current

•

Could cause brownouts or instability

OSNMA-capable firmware

Galileo authentication support

Special firmware version for cryptographic validation

Understanding GPS Speed Units:
What is a "knot"?
A knot is a unit of speed used in maritime and aviation navigation.
Definition:
•

1 knot = 1 nautical mile per hour

•

1 nautical mile = 1.852 kilometers = 1.15078 statute miles

Why "knot"?
The name comes from old sailing ships:
Sailors would throw a rope overboard with knots tied at regular intervals
They counted how many knots passed through their hands in a fixed time
More knots = faster speed
Hence: "making 10 knots" = traveling at 10 nautical miles per hour
Why use knots instead of km/h or mph?
1.
2.
3.
4.

1. Nautical miles match Earth's geometry:
o

1 nautical mile = 1 minute of latitude (1/60th of a degree)

o

Makes navigation calculations simpler on charts

o

Directly relates to degrees on a map

2. Universal in marine/aviation:
o

International standard for ships and aircraft

o

Charts and maps use nautical miles

o

Air traffic control uses knots

1. What is NMEA?
NMEA stands for National Marine Electronics Association. Originally a US marine electronics standards body,
NMEA created the NMEA 0183 standard which defines a simple text-based protocol for communication
between GNSS receivers and other devices.
NMEA 0183 is the universal language that virtually all GPS and GNSS receivers use to output their data.
When the Waveshare LC29H sends data to the Tenstar ESP32-S3, it uses NMEA 0183 sentences.
Key characteristics of NMEA 0183:
• Plain text format — every sentence is human-readable ASCII characters
• One sentence per line — each ends with a carriage return and line feed (CR+LF)
• Standard baud rate — 9600 baud by default, though many modern receivers (including the LC29H) use
115200
• Checksum — every sentence ends with a two-character hexadecimal checksum for error detection

The checksum is calculated by XOR-ing (exclusive OR) all the bytes between the $ and the * characters (not
including either). The result is expressed as two uppercase hexadecimal digits.
Example: $GPGGA,...,data...*4F

XOR means: if the bits are different the result is 1, if the same the result is
0.
This detects any single-bit transmission error.

Result is two hexadecimal digits (00-FF).

TinyGPSPlus validates checksums automatically.

1. What is GNSS?
GNSS stands for Global Navigation Satellite System. It is the general term for any satellite-based system that
provides positioning, navigation, and timing (PNT) services anywhere on Earth. GPS is one example of a
GNSS — Galileo is another.
A GNSS system works by having a constellation of satellites in orbit broadcasting precise timing signals. A
receiver on the ground listens to signals from multiple satellites simultaneously and uses the differences in
signal arrival times to calculate its own position. This technique is called trilateration.
At least four satellites must be visible for a full 3D position fix (latitude, longitude, and altitude). With more
satellites visible, accuracy improves.

2. Galileo — History and Context
Galileo is Europe's own civil global navigation satellite system, developed independently of the US GPS
system. The key motivations for Europe developing its own system were:
• Independence — GPS is controlled by the US military and could theoretically be degraded or switched
off for non-US users
• Civilian control — Galileo is under civilian governance, not military
• Higher accuracy — Galileo was designed from the outset with higher accuracy for civilian users
• Commercial and economic interest — Europe wanted to participate in the growing GNSS market

3. The Galileo Constellation
Galileo satellites orbit in Medium Earth Orbit (MEO) at an altitude of approximately 23,222 km. This is much
higher than the International Space Station (~400 km) but much lower than geostationary satellites (~36,000
km).
• Orbital planes: 3 (equally spaced around the Earth)
• Satellites per plane: 10 (planned) — approximately 9-10 operational at any time
• Total operational satellites: approximately 28-30
• Orbital inclination: 56 degrees (tilted relative to the equator)
• Orbital period: approximately 14 hours 4 minutes
The MEO orbit means each satellite is visible from a given location on Earth for several hours at a time, and
the constellation provides continuous global coverage.
Each Galileo satellite carries extremely precise atomic clocks:
• Passive Hydrogen Maser (PHM) — accuracy better than 1 nanosecond per day
• Rubidium Atomic Frequency Standard (RAFS) — backup clock
These clocks are essential because a timing error of just 1 nanosecond causes a position error of
approximately 30 centimetres.

5. OSNMA — Navigation Message Authentication
OSNMA stands for Open Service Navigation Message Authentication. It is one of Galileo's most significant
technical innovations and directly relevant to BIP26.
The problem OSNMA solves: a malicious actor could broadcast fake GPS or GNSS signals (called spoofing)
to make a receiver calculate an incorrect position. This is a serious security concern for critical infrastructure,
autonomous vehicles, and precision timing systems.
OSNMA allows a receiver to verify that the navigation messages it is receiving genuinely come from authentic
Galileo satellites and have not been tampered with.

5.1 How OSNMA Works
OSNMA uses a cryptographic protocol called TESLA (Timed Efficient Stream Loss-tolerant Authentication).
The key concepts are:

Galileo satellites broadcast authentication keys and digital signatures alongside the navigation data on
the E1-B signal
The receiver accumulates these keys and signatures over time
Once a key is disclosed (after a short delay), the receiver can verify that all previous data signed with
that key was authentic
The root of trust is a public key pre-installed in receiver firmware — this allows the chain of
authentication to be verified without any external connection

OSNMA authenticates navigation messages (not just the signal). It tells you the data came from a
real Galileo satellite.
OSNMA is broadcast on the Galileo E1-B signal frequency (1575.42 MHz).
OSNMA does not improve positioning accuracy — it improves trustworthiness.

Using two frequencies (dual-frequency reception) allows a receiver to measure and correct for ionospheric

7. Galileo Ground Infrastructure
The ground segment of Galileo consists of:
• Galileo Control Centres (GCC) — two centres in Oberpfaffenhofen, Germany and Fucino, Italy. These
monitor and control the entire satellite constellation.
• Galileo Sensor Stations (GSS) — a worldwide network of monitoring stations that track satellite signals
and feed data back to the control centres
• Telemetry, Tracking and Command (TT&C) stations — communicate directly with satellites for orbit
maintenance and clock corrections
• Up-link stations — broadcast navigation data and corrections up to the satellites
